FAQs

Straightforward answers to common reader questions, with links to the sources behind them.

These are common editorial topics, not a ranking based on search-volume data.

What is ADHD?

A neurodevelopmental condition involving persistent inattention, hyperactivity or impulsivity that affects daily functioning. It starts in childhood and may continue into adulthood.

Sources: CDC: ADHD in Children

Can I have ADHD without obvious hyperactivity?

Yes. Some people mainly have inattentive symptoms. A person does not need to be visibly restless to need an assessment.

Sources: CDC: Symptoms of ADHD

Does occasional distraction mean ADHD?

No. Assessment looks for a persistent, impairing pattern across settings and considers other explanations. A relatable symptom list is not a diagnosis.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD — What You Need to Know

Can an online test diagnose me?

No. Questionnaires may help organize concerns, but a diagnosis requires a clinical assessment. These website tools do not calculate an ADHD score.

Sources: NICE: ADHD diagnosis and management

Can ADHD be diagnosed in adulthood?

Yes. Some people were not recognized earlier. The assessment still explores symptoms from childhood, alongside current difficulties.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD in Adults — 4 Things to Know

Does ADHD look the same in every woman or girl?

No single pattern describes all girls and women. Inattentive difficulties can be overlooked, but any presentation can occur.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD — What You Need to Know

Could anxiety, poor sleep or another condition explain this?

Yes. Several conditions can resemble ADHD, and some can occur alongside it. A careful assessment considers both possibilities.

Sources: CDC: Diagnosing ADHD

Is ADHD caused by bad parenting?

ADHD is not a judgment on parenting. Genes play an important role and its causes are complex. Parent training provides useful support skills.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD — What You Need to Know

Do all children grow out of ADHD?

No. Symptoms may change with age and can continue into adult life. Support needs should be reviewed as circumstances change.

Sources: CDC: ADHD in Children

Is medication the only treatment?

No. Care may include psychological or behavioral support, parent training, school or workplace changes and medication, according to individual needs.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD — What You Need to Know

Which medicine is best?

There is no best choice for everyone. A qualified prescriber considers age, health, other medicines, response and side effects. This site does not recommend doses.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD — What You Need to Know

Can ADHD be cured?

There is currently no cure, but evidence-based care can reduce symptoms and improve functioning. Be cautious about products promising a guaranteed cure.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD — What You Need to Know

What can I ask a school to try?

Discuss clear instructions, organizational support, movement breaks and fewer distractions. Agree on individual goals and review progress. Formal entitlements differ by country.

Sources: CDC: ADHD in the Classroom

What can help at work or university?

Written instructions, a quieter space and help structuring tasks may be useful. Discuss what fits your role; local policies and legal rights vary.

Sources: NHS: ADHD in adults

What should I bring to an appointment?

Bring examples of difficulties, when they began, relevant health and medication information, and available childhood records. Ask the clinic what else it needs.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD in Adults — 4 Things to Know

When should I seek qualified help?

Seek an assessment when persistent difficulties affect learning, work, relationships or daily life. You can ask for help before knowing whether ADHD is the explanation.

Sources: NHS: ADHD in adults

Need urgent help?

If you or someone else is in immediate danger or may act on thoughts of self-harm, contact local emergency services now or go to the nearest emergency department. Ask a trusted person to stay with you while you get help. For distress without immediate danger, contact a local qualified health professional promptly. This website is not an emergency service.

Sources: WHO: Suicide questions and answers
