Children and teenagers

Children’s needs change as schoolwork and social expectations grow. Difficulties may involve following instructions, waiting, organizing materials or friendships. Assessment considers development and information from caregivers and school. Support should build skills and reduce barriers. Parent training and school-based support can be important parts of care; recommending parent training does not mean a parent caused ADHD.

Describe what happens before interpreting it

Practical application

Many children are active or distractible, especially when tired or under pressure. Concern grows when difficulties persist and disrupt learning, relationships or everyday activities. Notice the situation, the demand and what support was available. “The homework remained unfinished after repeated reminders” is more useful than “They are lazy.” Invite the child’s own explanation and avoid using a checklist to label them.

Sources: NHS: ADHD in children and young people

Bring home and school into the conversation

Original practical example

Ask the school what it observes and which supports have already been tried. A clinician can consider these observations alongside developmental and health history. Agree on a manageable goal rather than trying to change every difficulty at once. For example, trial a short written sequence for packing a bag, with help at the point it is needed. Review whether it reduces forgotten materials rather than expecting immediate independence.

Sources: NHS: ADHD in children and young people · CDC: ADHD in the Classroom

What parent training is for

Evidence summary

Evidence-based parent training teaches caregivers ways to create structure, use positive reinforcement and respond consistently. It involves learning and practice, often with feedback from a trained therapist. It is a treatment approach, particularly important for young children, not an accusation that parenting caused ADHD. Ask whether the programme has evidence for your child’s age, how caregivers are involved, and how it will fit the family’s language and circumstances.

Sources: CDC: Parent Training in Behavior Management

Keep the young person involved

Practical application

As children grow, ask what feels useful, embarrassing, tiring or unfair about the support plan. Make room for strengths, friendships and enjoyable activities alongside school goals. Parents and teachers should share relevant observations rather than assume every difficulty is ADHD. If sleep, mood, learning or safety concerns arise, tell the healthcare team. Ask early how care and support will continue when the young person changes schools or moves toward adult services.

Sources: NIMH: ADHD — What You Need to Know · NICE: ADHD diagnosis and management

A practical next step

Ask the child what feels hardest, then agree on one practical school or home support.

Need urgent help?

If you or someone else is in immediate danger or may act on thoughts of self-harm, contact local emergency services now or go to the nearest emergency department. Ask a trusted person to stay with you while you get help. For distress without immediate danger, contact a local qualified health professional promptly. This website is not an emergency service.
